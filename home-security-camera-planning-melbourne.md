# How to Choose Home Security Cameras in Melbourne: A Practical Planning Guide

Choosing a home security camera system starts with the property, not the camera count. Before comparing models, decide what each view needs to show, how the scene changes after dark, and how you will find and save recordings later.

## 1. Give each camera a job

Sketch the house and mark the front door, driveway, side access, garage and rear entry. For each possible camera position, write down the subject you want to see and its approximate distance: a visitor at the door, a vehicle entering the driveway, or movement along a side path.

A wide view can provide useful context, while a separate view aimed at an entrance may show more detail about a visitor. A digital zoom cannot restore detail that a camera did not capture. Think about the approach and departure routes, not only the place where you expect movement to occur.

Note anything that may block the image, including eaves, trees, gates and parked vehicles. If you can, take photographs from each proposed mounting position during the day and after dark.

## 2. Match the lens and mounting position to the view

The lens and image sensor determine the camera's field of view. A broad view covers more area; a narrower view makes a smaller area more prominent. Neither is automatically better. Choose based on the camera-to-subject distance and the detail you need from that location.

Record the proposed mounting height and distance to the subject. Ask to see the published viewing angle for the exact model and lens, then compare how the planned view frames a person approaching the property. Similar product names may have different lenses or model suffixes, so keep the complete model number with your shortlist.

## 3. Plan for the night scene

Night performance depends on the scene as well as the camera. Porch lights, headlights, reflective walls and sudden changes from a bright area to a dark one can affect the image. Review the actual location after dark and consider where illumination will fall.

Some models offer infrared, white light or modes that change with detected activity. Check what triggers each mode and whether visible light or audio alerts are suitable for the location. A setting that works for a driveway may be intrusive beside a bedroom window or a neighbour's property.

## 4. Decide how the system will record

Compare the recorder, storage and recording settings along with the cameras. Confirm the recorder model, its camera capacity, and whether the quoted kit includes a hard drive. If a listing says “No HDD,” storage still needs to be selected before the system can retain recordings.

Storage duration varies with camera count, resolution, bitrate, recording schedule and scene activity. Ask for an estimate based on the exact cameras and settings rather than assuming a drive size guarantees a particular number of days. Also check how to search for an event, export a clip and manage access for household members.

## 5. Choose wired, wireless or a combination

A wired network camera may use one Ethernet cable for data and power, and can be a good fit where cable routes are practical. A wireless camera can simplify installation in a difficult location, but still needs suitable power, dependable wireless coverage and a recording method that fits your needs. Battery cameras also need charging or an appropriate solar setup and may record differently from systems designed for continuous recording.

Walk the property and check wireless signal where cameras are planned. Decide whether you want continuous recording, event clips or a combination, and whether recordings will be stored locally, in the cloud or both. Check any subscription requirements, remote-viewing options and behaviour if the internet connection is unavailable.

## 6. Make quotes comparable

Give an installer the address, a property sketch, proposed mounting positions, building height, roof or ceiling access, likely cable routes and recorder location. Mention detached structures, multiple storeys, finished surfaces and any existing equipment or cabling you hope to reuse.

Ask each quote to list the camera and recorder models, included storage, cabling, mounting hardware, configuration, remote-viewing setup and handover. Keep optional equipment and any ongoing fees on separate lines. Two systems with the same camera count can have different equipment and installation scopes.

## 7. Bring a useful brief to the showroom

Before visiting a showroom, bring your sketch, day and night photos, existing camera or recorder model numbers, and the recording period you hope to achieve. This makes it easier to compare options against the actual property instead of choosing by camera count alone.

## Quick planning checklist

- Mark the entrances and approaches you want each camera to cover.
- Record approximate distances, mounting heights and possible obstructions.
- Review the real night scene and lighting conditions.
- Select the lens, recorder and storage as one system.
- Decide how footage will be recorded, reviewed and exported.
- Compare the complete installation scope, including optional and recurring costs.
